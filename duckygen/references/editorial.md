# Long-form editorial direction

Think in chapters, shots and actions as separate scales. A chapter may last 30–90 seconds, a shot commonly 3–5 seconds, and an action a few tenths of a second. Change the explanation's visual state at a meaningful spoken noun/verb: the browser opens a page, an authorization passes a boundary, a wallet drains, a signature is checked, a file becomes a searchable index. Every illustrated action must be implemented, not merely written in JSON.

## Keep one recognizable visual world

Record a compact `art_direction` in the project plan: palette/color meanings, font roles, line weight and texture, shape language, lighting/depth, recurring object, captions, camera intent and transitions. Reuse these decisions across the whole film and future revisions unless the user requests a new direction. Default to the light paper/ink/coral sketch style. New assets should match its stroke quality and level of detail. Do not drift between glossy 3D, stock-photo collage, pixel art and outline icons simply to increase variety. Source evidence can keep its authentic appearance within the established film framing.

Create variety through scale, angle, reveal, spatial relationship and cause/effect. Carry the same object and accent meanings through close-ups, wide shots and maps. Green should keep its established meaning; do not use it for danger in one scene and resolution in the next. Keep captions and typography roles consistent. A scene can feel new without changing the film's identity.

## Attention and comprehension

Think about what the viewer knows, wants to know and needs to see next. Use these practical editorial choices rather than unsupported promises about retention:

- Curiosity: establish a specific unanswered question and show why the answer matters. Pay off that question instead of chaining vague teasers.
- Orientation: introduce the actors and spatial relationship before a complex transformation. Use the recurring object as a visual memory anchor.
- Cognitive load: present one dominant action or comparison at a time. Reveal secondary details after the main idea lands; do not make viewers read a paragraph while decoding a new mechanism.
- Causality: show the trigger, the visible change and its consequence. Labels alone do not demonstrate a mechanism.
- Expectation and contrast: use a truthful reversal, scale comparison or close-up when it changes understanding. Avoid arbitrary shake, fabricated urgency, startling volume jumps or sensationalized evidence.
- Rhythm: alternate explanation and payoff. Let an important figure or difficult idea settle; tighten empty lead-ins, repeated copy and dead air. Keep speech natural.
- Closure: resolve the opening question and leave a concrete takeaway. Echo the recurring object or an earlier composition to make the ending feel earned.

Add concise `viewer_question`, `payoff` and `music_state` notes to meaningful story beats in the existing plan. These are editorial aids, not a second script. Review the rough cut at small size: can a viewer identify the main action, understand the comparison, and read the caption before the next shot? Remove competing layers before adding more movement.

## Film composition rather than page layout

Design a moving full-frame stage. Use foreground/background, large objects, reveals, crop, camera distance and off-screen entrances to guide the eye. An explanation should progress within the shot, not just swap text inside a repeating card. Avoid persistent mastheads, hero sections, rounded card grids, navigation, scrolling-page tours and title/icon/paragraph stacks. Keep player controls outside the export. Actual product UI is appropriate when it is the subject: inspect it, isolate the relevant interaction and move its layers with deliberate camera framing. A functional button inside a truthful UI demonstration is different from decorating every scene with web UI.

Inspect a contact sheet for repeated layout and style drift, then inspect motion for meaningful actions, readable holds and clean handoffs. A page-like frame with a tiny animated accent is still a weak film shot. Recompose it around the actor or mechanism; adding more transitions will not fix its staging.

Choose a recurring object that explains the topic: request packet, document, key, signal or data point. Carry it through related setups. Alternate wide setup, close-up, causal diagram, contrast, evidence, consequence, then return to the central mechanism. Avoid repeatedly arranging a title at top-left and a small icon at right. Give the object enough screen area to explain the action; large labels carry fewer words, not paragraphs.

A hook should establish a concrete tension or question quickly. Open the mechanism promptly rather than spending a chapter on decorative scene-setting. Use mini-payoffs and chapter questions to sustain a longer video. Add context because the audience needs it, not to fill a target length. Do not force 120 BPM, constant motion, or a half-second maximum hold onto narration. Readable stable holds and natural emphasis are part of good editing.

For the user's reference style, use paper/ink contrast, imperfect-but-stable line work, coral accents, original sketch props and occasional carefully placed green. The provided images inspire line quality and composition. They do not authorize copying a mascot, brand asterisk or exact drawing. The pixel-art examples do not require pixel art throughout. Use actual subject-relevant assets.

A useful plan entry is:

```
start/end | spoken idea / viewer question | asset IDs/evidence | composition | action/anchor + payoff | camera | transition | cue/contact time | music state
```

Write detailed micro-timing for the actions that require it, not a repetitive paragraph per frame. Store the plan once and use it for audio, captions, drawing and render reviews. If an action/asset changes, update the shared plan and affected shot rather than regenerating the film.

Use small shake only for a deliberately strong impact (roughly 2–6 screen pixels, 80–160ms, analytically damped). Do not shake narration-heavy text. Contrast/scale/masking or an audio hit is often better. No fixed quota of shake, zooms, icons or effects.

Read the actual supplied footage/reference when available. If a link cannot be downloaded, describe the evidence limit and work from supplied stills and written direction. Treat source branding as evidence to preserve, not an invitation to fabricate a product interface.
